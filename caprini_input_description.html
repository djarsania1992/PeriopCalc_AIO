Caprini Input Definitions

Text

Detailed definitions for each Caprini risk factor used in the calculator.

Definitions

Age

VTE is rare in children and young adults unless strong predisposing risk factors are present, such as cancer, trauma, indwelling lines, systemic infection, or family history of thrombosis. Patients over 40 years of age remain at significantly increased risk compared to younger patients, and the risk approximately doubles with each subsequent decade.

Type of surgery

Minor surgery is defined by anesthesia length less than 45 minutes and does not refer to surgical complexity. Major surgery includes procedures with general or regional anesthesia longer than 45 minutes, including open, laparoscopic, or arthroscopic cases.

Reoperations during the same hospitalization count for 2 points each if the anesthesia time exceeds 45 minutes. Major lower extremity arthroplasties are high-risk procedures when additional risk factors are present. Once the Caprini score reaches ≥10, overall VTE risk is significantly greater. If the procedure is performed because of a hip fracture, preoperative VTE screening may be beneficial.

Major surgery (within one month)

Major surgery performed during the preceding month includes recent staged procedures. This factor captures the increased VTE risk associated with recent major surgical trauma.

CHF

The VTE risk is similar for patients with preserved or reduced ejection fraction. A variety of clinical criteria may fit this risk factor.

Sepsis

This factor is defined as an infection requiring IV antibiotics. Infection promotes thrombosis through endothelial injury, tissue factor-induced activation of the procoagulant pathway, down-regulation of the endogenous anticoagulant pathway, and inhibition of fibrinolysis.

Pneumonia

Preoperative pneumonia is a significant VTE risk factor. Postoperative pneumonia is also associated with elevated VTE risk. Do not count an additional point for infection unless another separate source of infection is identified.

Immobilizing plaster cast

This includes any device that limits ankle motion and interferes with calf muscle pumping action. Patients who are not weight-bearing are also at risk because baseline blood flow does not improve.

Hip, pelvis, or leg fracture

These fractures increase VTE risk, and the degree of risk varies with fracture location. The incidence of pulmonary embolism may occur with equal frequency for above-knee and below-knee thrombosis. Temporary IVC filters may be used to prevent emboli depending on clinical circumstances.

Stroke

Stroke is a leading cause of death and often leads to VTE. A paradoxical embolus can travel from a DVT through a PFO into the systemic circulation, causing thrombotic stroke. A history of atrial fibrillation may also cause embolic stroke.

Thrombotic strokes in the past should be counted as previous thrombosis and scored as 3 points.

Multiple trauma

This includes patients with multiple injuries involving fractures, severe chest and abdominal contusions, and internal injuries. Venous thromboembolism increases mortality in these patients, and prophylaxis is essential. If bleeding issues prevent anticoagulation, surveillance and/or temporary vena cava filters may be used.

Acute spinal cord injury causing paralysis

Patients may not report symptoms due to nerve damage. Initial VTE presentation can include extensive thrombosis or death. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach.

Varicose veins

Visible bulging veins are a risk factor for thrombosis. This does not include spider veins or telangiectasia. Risk is especially increased in cancer patients. The idea that varicose veins are harmless is not well founded.

Current swollen legs

Swollen legs include pitting edema, loss of bony prominence definition, obscured surface foot veins, or indentation after removal of a stocking. This factor refers to one or both legs. The physical exam should include pretibial pressure to assess for indentation.

Current central venous access

Central venous access includes any catheter in a blood vessel in the arm, neck, or chest that delivers blood or medicine directly to the heart within the last month. Examples include central lines, ports, and PICC lines.

History of DVT, PE, or thrombotic stroke

Each episode of venous thrombosis in any location, as well as pulmonary emboli, is included. The combination of DVT and PE remains three points. Subsequent surgical procedures in a patient with thrombotic history are associated with a very high incidence of thrombosis, and extended prophylaxis for up to one month may be indicated.

Family history of thrombosis

This is one of the most powerful risk factors for thrombosis and is often not asked or included in some risk models. Increased risk has been documented in first-, second-, and third-degree relatives. This includes any DVT, PE, or thrombotic stroke.

Other congenital or acquired thrombophilia

Thrombophilia is a congenital or acquired imbalance in hemostasis that produces a hypercoagulable state. A family member with a history of VTE scores 3 points. If that family member has a positive thrombophilia marker, the patient score would be 6 points. Multiple thrombophilia markers together increase VTE potential and are scored as 3 points for each factor.

Mobility

Bed rest is defined as not being able to walk 30 feet (10 meters) at one time. Bathroom privileges or walking only in the room are not considered ambulation. Walking this distance reduces VTE risk by about 50%.

History of inflammatory bowel disease

This includes regional ileitis and ulcerative colitis, but not irritable bowel syndrome. Patients with inactive as well as active disease should be included. Thrombotic risk is increased during active disease, and extended postoperative prophylaxis may benefit patients with acute disease.

BMI >25

This factor comes from multiple sources, including criteria associated with readmission after total joint replacement and higher risk in patients taking birth control pills.

Acute MI

This risk factor is associated with a transient increased VTE risk for the first six months, independent of traditional atherosclerotic risk factors. The strength of the risk decreases after three months.

COPD

COPD is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and is characterized by systemic inflammation. Up to 30% of these patients can develop venous thromboembolism. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis combined with COPD carries even higher VTE risk.

Present or previous malignancy

This factor excludes basal cell skin cancer except melanoma. Cancer is a major driver of VTE and is associated with higher mortality and decreased quality of life in affected patients.

Positive Factor V Leiden

The most common inherited gene mutation is rarely seen in Asian or African populations. It is associated with increased VTE risk, especially in the homozygous form. It involves resistance of Factor V and increases the chance of arterial or venous thrombosis. A synergistic VTE risk exists when combined with other risk factors.

Positive prothrombin 20210A

The second most common inherited gene mutation in Caucasians involves elevated plasma coagulation factor II, which increases venous thrombosis risk, especially in the rare homozygous form. Combined heterozygosity for Factor V Leiden and prothrombin gene mutation produces thrombotic risk similar to a single homozygous defect.

HIT

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is an immune reaction to heparin that can result in thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. It is much less common with LMWH compared to unfractionated heparin.

MTHFR

MTHFR is an enzyme important in folate metabolism. Genetic variants, especially C677T, have been linked to thrombotic events when combined with another defect. A deficiency may result in hyperhomocysteinemia, although elevated homocysteine has not been firmly proven to cause venous thrombosis. Many patients carry these variants, and this area remains controversial.

APS

Antiphospholipid syndrome is associated with venous and arterial thrombosis, especially obstetrical complications. Laboratory defects include LA, ACA, and B2 glycoprotein antibodies. Patients may remain positive long after the clinical event.

Other risk factors (Not validated in the original model)

BMI >40

Even physically fit or athletic individuals can be at risk when they are very large. Excess body size increases cardiovascular stress, which may lead to cardiac hypertrophy, hypertension, valvular disease, sleep apnea, and insulin-requiring diabetes. VTE events rise linearly with weight, and patients with BMI >35 have a sixfold increase in events. Risk increases further with BMI >40.

Blood transfusion

The infusion of 1–2 units of blood older than 28 days raises DVT rates and mortality in trauma patients. DVT incidence is also increased after ventral hernia and bariatric surgery in patients receiving transfusions, with many events occurring after discharge. Cancer patients receiving RBC transfusions have higher VTE rates.

Smoking

Smoking includes inhalation of any burned substance, including tobacco, marijuana, or vaping. Smokers are more likely to develop postoperative VTE than never smokers.

Diabetes mellitus requiring insulin

Patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes have higher rates of DVT and PE than controls, and both groups are at increased risk for recurrent VTE.

Human immunodeficiency virus

HIV activates inflammatory, immunologic, and coagulation pathways, increasing VTE risk.

Chemotherapy

VTE incidence rises in patients treated with chemotherapy. Incidental VTE is relatively common in patients with solid tumors, especially in the first months of treatment. Multiple myeloma patients treated with thalidomide or lenalidomide plus dexamethasone and/or chemotherapy have particularly elevated risk. Neoadjuvant cancer therapy also increases VTE risk. Current ASCO guidelines address chemotherapy use across many cancer scenarios.

SVT

Superficial vein thrombosis is a VTE risk factor, especially with other risk factors. SVT combined with an acquired thrombotic risk factor increases venous thrombosis risk 10- to 100-fold. Isolated SVT frequently progresses to DVT and PE. Cancer patients with SVT have a poor prognosis similar to those with DVT and high recurrence rates.
